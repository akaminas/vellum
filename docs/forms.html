Forms

Name the job of the data, then take the form

Seven families of data job, after the R Graph Gallery's index, each with its form and its rules. Colour follows the job: identity takes the tones in order, magnitude takes one ramp, polarity takes the diverging pair, state takes status.

0. Is it a figure at all?

One number with a comparison is a sentence in the text or a stat tile on a slide, not a one-bar chart. A two-slice pie is a sentence. A table beats a chart when the reader will look values up rather than see a pattern.

73.2years of life expected at birth, worldwide, 2023against 46.4 in 1950
−1.7years lost between 2019 and 2021recovered by 2023

1. Change over time

Line chart, the default

Tones in slot order at 1.6 pt, ringed end dots, end labels. Up to four series without a legend. Uncertainty as a tint band at 0.55 opacity with a quiet note saying what the band is. Member runs as 0.7 pt lines in the tone with the mean on top. Time on x, always. Counts start at zero; for a rate or an index, the unit line says where the axis starts. A log axis gets a note.

Area and stacked area

Tints with a 1.2 pt surface gap between layers, the slot order from the bottom, each layer labelled at its right end in its deep step. Stack only parts of a whole. A streamgraph is allowed on a poster or a slide for a whole that drifts in time, and says that its baseline is centred.

Slope and bump charts

Two moments: a slope chart, tones for the series the figure is about, rule grey for the rest, labels at both ends. Ranks over time: a bump chart with one series in terracotta and the others in rule grey.

2. Comparison and ranking

Bar chart

Horizontal when the labels are words, vertical when the categories are ordered. One series takes one colour: the teal tint for the many, the terracotta tone for the one highlighted. The value at the bar end in ink when the figure is about the values. Sorted by value unless the order means something. Error bars in secondary, no caps. Never colour nominal bars by their value; never a bar chart of one bar.

Dot plot, lollipop and dumbbell

A dot plot replaces a bar chart with more than about twelve categories: a ringed dot at the value on a 0.7 pt rule-grey stem. Two values per category, before and after, are two dots joined by a stem: the dumbbell.

Radar, circular bar, parallel coordinates

Not used for comparison. A circular form only when the angle means something: a month, an hour, a compass bearing. Several variables per item go in a table or in small multiples of bars.

3. Distribution

Histogram and density

A histogram in the tint with a 1.2 pt surface gap between bars, one series only. Two or more distributions as density curves in the tones with the area as the tint at 0.3 opacity, or as a ridgeline: one row per group, the tone line over the tint area, rows in slot order from the top. The subtitle says the bin width or bandwidth.

Box, violin, beeswarm

Fewer than about thirty points: show them, as a beeswarm of ringed dots. Thirty to a few hundred: a box plot in the tone with the box filled in the tint, whiskers 0.7 pt. Many: a violin in the tint with the tone outline. The unit line says what the box and the whiskers are.

4. Relation

Scatter plot

Ringed dots. Up to three families without labels, because the first three tones pass every pair, not only neighbours; more with direct labels. A predicted-against-observed scatter carries the identity line in rule grey with "y = x" written along it. A fit line in the tone at 1.6 pt with its band in the tint. Text labels on points only for the points the claim names.

Heat map and 2D density

One ramp, teal by default, or the lagoon gradient when the values span many decades or the lowest cells must stay visible. Cells separated by 1.2 pt surface lines, no spines, a legend bar without an outline. Values inside cells only where they change the reading. A failed or missing cell is hatched in the terracotta tone on the card colour. The axis ticks are the parameter values, not indices.

Connected scatter and correlogram

A connected scatter for a path in two variables over time: a 1.6 pt line with ringed dots and the years labelled every few steps. A correlogram as a half matrix on the diverging scale, the midpoint at zero.

5. Part of a whole

A single part of a whole is a horizontal stacked bar of tints with surface gaps, the labels inside the segments in the deep step where they fit. A waffle, ten by ten, for a share the reader should count. A treemap only for a hierarchy; a Voronoi treemap is a poster and report form, never a paper form. A pie or doughnut only up to six segments, at a glance, with the shares written on them, and never for close values.

6. Position: maps

Land in the card colour or the classes, borders in the grid colour at 0.4 pt, water as the surface. A choropleth takes one ramp with its classes stated in the legend (quantiles or equal steps), and countries without data hatched. A bubble map takes ringed dots sized by area, with three reference sizes in the legend. No basemap tiles, no relief, no labels except the places the claim names.

Small countries vanish on a world map, so a dot plot of the same values goes beside it. A 3D tilted map is a terrain figure, not a Vellum figure: draw the map flat and colour the value.

7. Flow, trees and networks

Phylogeny and tree

Branches 1.0 pt in secondary, tips as ringed dots, the clades the figure is about in the tones (first clade teal, second terracotta) and the rest in secondary. A time axis at the bottom in muted with the present at the right. No node circles, no support values unless the claim is about support.

Network

Edges 0.4 pt in rule grey at 0.6 opacity, nodes as ringed dots in the tones by community, up to six communities. A highlighted node and its neighbours in terracotta, the rest faded to the grid colour. The subtitle says how the layout was made.

Sankey and chord

Flows in tints at 0.6 opacity, nodes as bars in the tones, labels in ink on the nodes. A chord diagram only as small multiples when there are more than about eight groups.

The third axis

A 3D axis is used only when three measured quantities form one shape and the shape is the claim. Every other third dimension is a colour, a size, a panel or a contour line. Four questions, in order:

  1. Are x, y and z three quantities of equal standing, so that z = f(x, y)? If z is a category, a count per group or a time, it is not a third axis.
  2. Is the shape the claim: a ridge, a trough, a saddle, the place of a peak, several optima?
  3. Can the reader do the task from a heat map with contour lines? If yes, stop here and draw that.
  4. If yes to 1 and 2 and no to 3, draw the 3D figure, and add its 2D twin.
settingvalue, and why
projectionorthographic: perspective makes the near edge look larger, which is false for data
viewelevation 24°, azimuth −58°: shows the shape and the floor, neither floor axis foreshortened
box aspect1 : 1 : 0.62
panestransparent, hairline edges and grid in the grid colour, three ticks per axis
colourrepeats z with a named gradient; flat shading, no light source, because a lit surface changes colour with its slope
meshnone; faces about 1 % of the width (90 × 90); a wireframe only when the shape must be seen through
floor6 % below the minimum, with seven contour lines in the rule colour
filethe surface rasterised at 200 dpi or more, the text vector

Every 3D figure has a 2D twin

A heat map with contour lines of the same data, the same gradient, the same limits, the same axes, as panel b or in the supplement. The twin is where a value is read; the 3D panel is where the shape is seen. On the web a rotatable version is allowed beside the static one: try it.

Never 3D

3D bars, 3D pies, 3D areas, extruded or ribbon lines, a perspective tilt on a flat chart, a shadow, a bevel, a reflection. These add area and distortion that encode nothing and make the front bars look bigger than the back ones. In a pitch deck, a report or an infographic, no 3D at all: a reader who is not a specialist reads depth as decoration.

Across all forms

  1. Identity takes the tones in slot order; magnitude takes one ramp; polarity takes the diverging pair; state takes status.
  2. Highlight one, grey the rest, when the claim is about one thing. Terracotta is the highlight; rule grey is the rest.
  3. Facet into small multiples before adding a seventh colour or a second axis.
  4. Every form has its direct labels and its quiet note saying what the marks are.
  5. The claim decides the form. A form the reader cannot name needs a sentence in the subtitle that says how to read it.
