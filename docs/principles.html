Principles

The claim decides the form, and the form decides the colour

Most bad figures start with a colour. A Vellum figure starts with one sentence, the claim, and the colour comes last. This page gives the recipe, the reasons, and where each rule came from.

The recipe

Seven steps, in this order. A person or an agent following them produces a Vellum figure without guessing.

  1. Write the claim in one sentence. That sentence is the title. If it needs two sentences, make two figures.
  2. Pick the form by the job of the data: change over time, comparison, distribution, relation, part of a whole, position on a map, a tree or a network. Chart rules by form.
  3. Pick the medium and take its sizes: width, type sizes, and whether the dark twin applies. Media.
  4. Load the palette through the tool's module and assign tones to the series in the fixed order. Never cycle past six: fold the rest into "other" or facet into small multiples.
  5. Build the anatomy: claim title, unit line, direct end labels, ringed dots, hairline grid, footer with the source and the git stamp. Anatomy of a figure.
  6. Run the checklist. Render the figure and look at it: the validator checks colour, not collisions. The checklist.
  7. Save as PDF for print and PNG at 300 dpi for screens, with the git stamp in the footer or the file name.

One number is a sentence, not a chart. "Life expectancy at birth was 73.2 years in 2023, against 46.4 in 1950" belongs in the text or on a stat tile. A two-slice pie is a sentence. A table beats a chart when the reader will look values up.

The removal test

Every decorative choice in Vellum is settled by one test. Remove the element and replace it with nothing, or with one flat colour. If the figure now carries less information, the element was data. If it carries the same information, delete it.

The test removes gradients that encode nothing, 3D tilts on flat data, drop shadows, bevels, outlines around bars and dots, boxes around plots, and legend boxes that repeat what end labels already say. It keeps a gradient that is a heat map's scale, a third axis that is the shape of a surface, and a hatch that marks a missing value.

  • a gradient as a page, card or slide background
  • a 3D effect on 2D data
  • a glow, shadow, bevel or reflection
  • an outline around a bar or a dot
  • a box around the plot
  • a legend box for four series or fewer

Lessons from Visual Cinnamon

Nadieh Bremer's portfolio at visualcinnamon.com was read piece by piece: seventeen commissions for the New York Times, Scientific American, UNESCO, Google Trends, the EU Publications Office and others. Each one taught something specific. The palette register, muted and warm with the ink dark and the accents earthy, comes from Searching for Birds. Her pieces are commissions with weeks of design time; Vellum keeps the habits that survive a one-hour figure.

Four more lessons worth keeping

A figure must read with the highlight off

The ORCA commit timeline works without anyone assigned. Highlight is a layer, not the structure.

Pick the form that shows the mechanism

Reach needs a network. Choose the form that shows how the claim works, not the form that shows the most data.

Say when position carries nothing

The EPFL city rings are placed for balance, not geography, and say so. Use meaningless position for legibility and write that down.

Draw the guide by hand, place the data by code

The NYT tracker swirl was drawn by hand because a programmed curve never looks natural; the data were placed along it by code.

Lessons from the galleries

The R Graph Gallery

The R Graph Gallery indexes about fifty chart types in seven families by the job of the data: distribution, correlation, ranking, part of a whole, evolution, maps, flow. Vellum's chart rules use the same seven families. Its featured examples share ten habits, which are the same ten found independently in Bremer's work: labels at line ends, inline labels instead of a legend, repelled labels, small multiples, insets, one group highlighted and the rest grey, one palette through a piece, a publication template as a model, whitespace, and a caption that carries the source.

Color Hunt

Forty palettes under the "pastel" tag on Color Hunt show what the public means by pastel. Three regularities: a pastel palette always has a cream rather than a white, it has one dark anchor so the light colours have something to sit against, and its pastels are tints, high in lightness and low in chroma, of hues spread around the wheel. Vellum's card is that cream, its ink is that anchor, and its tints are exactly that register (OKLCH lightness 0.84, chroma 0.05).

Canva's colour combinations

Canva's guide supplies the vocabulary Vellum uses: monochromatic, complementary, analogous, triadic and tetradic schemes; a tint is a hue plus white, a shade a hue plus black, a tone a hue plus grey; warm and cool are the two halves of the wheel. It says to experiment with "how much or how little of each colour". Vellum answers with a fixed proportion.

The proportion is the style

Most of the picture is surface. Then ink and muted text. Then tints as area, tones as line, and the deep step rarely. The hues matter less than how much of each is used.

Why four colours is not a chart palette

A Color Hunt palette is a mood for a web page: background, two mid tones, one dark. A chart needs a surface, text at three strengths, a grid, six distinguishable series, a sequential ramp, a diverging pair and a status set. vellum.json holds about ninety values for that reason, all derived from six hue angles and two neutrals.

The things that are never in a Vellum figure

  • a second y axis: two measures are two panels, or an index to a common base
  • a box around the plot
  • a dashed gridline
  • a rainbow colour scale
  • a pie with more than six slices
  • a drop shadow
  • an outline around a bar or a dot
  • a title that is a noun phrase
  • a decorative gradient: a gradient is a data scale or it is absent
  • a 3D effect on 2D data: 3D axes only for a surface or a 3D scatter, with a 2D twin
  • text in a series colour (identity comes from the mark beside the text)
  • a seventh series colour (fold, facet or grey out instead)

Scope

Everything: thesis figures and TikZ diagrams, modelling work, reports and policy briefs, posters, slides, pitch decks, infographics and the drawings in a notebook. The medium changes the sizes and the density. It never changes the palette, the fonts or the anatomy.

That constancy is the point. A set of figures made months apart, in four different tools, by different people, should still read as one body of work. The rules are written down and the colours are fixed in files so that this does not depend on anyone's memory or taste.
